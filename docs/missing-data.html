Bias of the Gini index under the MCAR, MAR and MNAR response mechanisms, and how to deal with it

Modified

November 17, 2026

Missing data are very common in inequality studies: people are less willing to answer sensitive questions such as their income, and those who do not answer are often different from those who do. This simulator, linked to objective OG2 of the project, shows how much the Gini index can be biased by missing data, depending on the response mechanism and on the method used to deal with the missing values. It runs a Monte Carlo experiment in your browser.

The three response mechanisms

MCAR · Missing completely at random
The probability of non-response is the same for everybody. The respondents are a random subsample of the sample.
MAR · Missing at random
Non-response depends on an observed variable x (for example, education, age or occupation) that is related to income, but not on income itself once x is known.
MNAR · Missing not at random
Non-response depends on the income itself: for example, people with very high (or very low) incomes are less willing to report it.

In the simulation, the probability that unit i does not respond follows a logistic model P(\text{missing}_i)=\{1+\exp(-\alpha-\beta\,v_i)\}^{-1}, where v_i=0 under MCAR, v_i=x_i under MAR and v_i is the (standardised) rank of y_i under MNAR. The intercept \alpha is set so that the expected percentage of missing data is the one chosen, and \beta is the strength of the mechanism. The auxiliary variable x is observed for everybody and its correlation with the (normal scores of) income is \rho.

How to read the results
  • Complete cases (deleting the units with missing data) is only safe under MCAR. Under MAR and MNAR the respondents are no longer representative, and if the richest people do not respond, inequality is underestimated.
  • Mean imputation always underestimates inequality, because all the imputed incomes are equal. It should never be used to estimate the Gini index.
  • Regression imputation also underestimates inequality, because it ignores the residual variability of income.
  • Stochastic regression imputation and multiple imputation add a random residual to the prediction. Under MAR, they remove most of the bias, provided that the imputation model includes the variable x that explains non-response. Multiple imputation also reflects the uncertainty due to imputation and has a smaller error.
  • Under MNAR, no standard method removes the bias. Sensitivity analyses and auxiliary information on the non-respondents are needed.
  • Increase the percentage of missing data, the strength of the mechanism or the inequality of the data to see how the problems grow; reduce the correlation \rho to see how imputation loses its advantage under MAR.

Methods compared

Methods for dealing with missing data.
Method What it does When it works
Complete cases Deletes the units with missing income MCAR only
Mean imputation Replaces each missing income with the mean of the respondents Never for inequality measures
Hot-deck imputation Replaces each missing income with the income of a randomly chosen respondent MCAR (or within classes that explain non-response)
Regression imputation Predicts \log y from x with a linear regression fitted to the respondents Not for inequality measures (ignores residual variability)
Stochastic regression imputation Prediction plus a residual drawn at random from the respondents’ residuals MAR, if the model includes the variables that explain non-response
Multiple imputation (M=5) Stochastic regression repeated M times, refitting the model on bootstrap samples of the respondents; the M Gini indices are averaged (Rubin’s rules) MAR; also gives valid variances and intervals

Good practice when estimating the Gini index with missing data. Report the percentage of missing data and analyse the response mechanism; never use mean imputation or simply delete the incomplete cases unless the data are MCAR; include in the imputation model the variables related to income and to non-response; use multiple imputation with enough imputations for the percentage of missing data; and report the uncertainty due to imputation.

The code of the simulator is in js/gini-sim.js. Estimator of the Gini index: \widehat{G}_n^{c}. Gamma is not included because the simulator generates the income and the auxiliary variable through a normal copula, which requires a closed-form quantile function.

## How to read the results
- **Complete cases** (deleting the units with missing data) is only safe under **MCAR**. Under MAR and MNAR the respondents are no longer representative, and if the richest people do not respond, inequality is underestimated.
- **Mean imputation** always underestimates inequality, because all the imputed incomes are equal. It should never be used to estimate the Gini index.
- **Regression imputation** also underestimates inequality, because it ignores the residual variability of income.
- **Stochastic regression imputation** and **multiple imputation** add a random residual to the prediction. Under **MAR**, they remove most of the bias, *provided that the imputation model includes the variable* $x$ *that explains non-response*. Multiple imputation also reflects the uncertainty due to imputation and has a smaller error.
- Under **MNAR**, no standard method removes the bias. Sensitivity analyses and auxiliary information on the non-respondents are needed.
- Increase the **percentage of missing data**, the **strength** of the mechanism or the **inequality** of the data to see how the problems grow; reduce the correlation $\rho$ to see how imputation loses its advantage under MAR.
:::

## Methods compared

| Method | What it does | When it works |
|---|---|---|
| Complete cases | Deletes the units with missing income | MCAR only |
| Mean imputation | Replaces each missing income with the mean of the respondents | Never for inequality measures |
| Hot-deck imputation | Replaces each missing income with the income of a randomly chosen respondent | MCAR (or within classes that explain non-response) |
| Regression imputation | Predicts $\log y$ from $x$ with a linear regression fitted to the respondents | Not for inequality measures (ignores residual variability) |
| Stochastic regression imputation | Prediction plus a residual drawn at random from the respondents' residuals | MAR, if the model includes the variables that explain non-response |
| Multiple imputation ($M=5$) | Stochastic regression repeated $M$ times, refitting the model on bootstrap samples of the respondents; the $M$ Gini indices are averaged (Rubin's rules) | MAR; also gives valid variances and intervals |
